# Worked examples — the same decision, written twice

Four pairs. Each bad version was really sent; each good version is the same decision
rewritten to the four elements. Read the pair, not the rule — the rule is in `SKILL.md`.

---

## 1. The policy corpus hold

The decision: fourteen drafted security policy and standard documents describe controls
that are not in place. Hold them from external use, or approve them with the gaps recorded
as a documented POA&M (Plan of Action and Milestones)?

**Bad**

> Row 83 (Thorne) remains Pending, DecisionRelevantBy 2026-09-28. Thorne's disagreement
> list is now 20 clauses (7 Critical, 8 High, 5 Medium), up from 14, none resolved. Row 63
> is the adjacent item and is already overdue. Please review and advise on disposition.

What is wrong: the unit of action is a row number; the decision itself is never stated in
words; the counts are the stack's bookkeeping, not his input; "advise on disposition" is
not a recommendation; the date is presented as a deadline and it is not one; and nothing
says what happens if he never answers.

**Good**

> Your fourteen security policy documents claim controls the company does not have in place.
> Recommendation: hold them from external use until the wording matches what is actually
> running. Say nothing and that is what happens — they stay internal and nothing goes to a
> customer or an auditor. No external deadline; the date on this item is our own review
> scheduling, not a filing.

All four elements, no identifier, and the default is safe because holding is the
recommendation. Note that *releasing* them would be a category-two item (sending something
outside the company) and would have waited for an explicit yes regardless.

## 2. The borrowed deadline

**Bad**

> The policy corpus decision is due 2026-09-28 and is now overdue against its own date.

Where 2026-09-28 came from: a solicitation's bid-opening date on an entirely unrelated
construction procurement, which had been carried through the registers for weeks. It was
attached to this item as a review-by date and then read back as a deadline by every pass
that followed, including this one. Nothing external required a decision on that day.

**Good**

> No external deadline. Our own review date on this item is 28 September; that is
> scheduling, not a clock, and I am not escalating against it. The one real date in this
> area is the bid opening on the federal construction solicitation, which is a watch item
> and not our obligation.

The rule this pair produces: **a date names its source or it is labeled internal.** A date
that cannot name a filing, a comment period, a counterparty, a statute, or a contract is
this stack's own scheduling, and calling it a deadline teaches Nick to discount the dates
that are real.

## 3. The options menu

**Bad**

> Three paths on the certification question: (a) pursue the full listing now, (b) pursue a
> component-level listing first and the system listing after funding, (c) defer both and
> revisit after the next raise. Each has trade-offs — (a) is fastest to a sellable claim
> but most expensive, (b) is cheapest per step but extends the timeline, (c) preserves cash
> but leaves the claim unsayable. Let me know which you'd like to pursue.

What is wrong: the analysis has been moved onto his desk with the weights left equal, and
the close is permission-seeking on work already in scope.

**Good**

> Recommendation: component-level listing first, system listing after the raise. It is the
> only path that buys a sayable claim before the money arrives, and the sequencing does not
> foreclose the full listing later. Doing nothing means neither claim is sayable to a
> customer through the end of the year. No external date forces this; the constraint is
> cash, not a calendar.

If the recommendation genuinely does not exist yet, the packet is not ready. Work it
further or decide it — do not hand over three options and call it a decision.

## 4. The sixty-eight-row queue

**Bad**

> 68 of 100 register rows are Pending — 28 GREEN, 38 YELLOW, 2 RED. Nothing reviewed since
> 11 September. Recommend a review pass on rows 14, 16, 17, 31, 35, 44, 45, 46, 57 …

Nick's own verdict on this format: *"This needs a better resolution path. I'm not going to
go into the SharePoint list."* He is right, and the interface is not the defect — the
inflow is.

**Good**

> The sixty-eight pending items are about six decisions, and four of them are standing
> rules you approve once rather than adjudications you repeat:
>
> - **Relationships** (12 items): the relationship seat records and prioritizes by its opportunity test,
>   and escalates only what needs money, a signature, or crosses an entity boundary.
> - **Claim checks** (9 items): approve the sayable set once; thereafter a pass is filed
>   and only a failure reaches you.
> - **Tenant and identity hygiene** (8 items): the seats fix or record; only your own
>   authority escalates.
> - **Bid or no-bid** (6 items): below the threshold and inside the lane, the scorecard
>   decides and the decision is recorded.
>
> Recommendation: approve those four rules. Say nothing and they take effect as written.
> That leaves four items that are genuinely yours, one line each in tomorrow's brief.
>
> The 28 GREEN rows are not decisions. They are records of acts you already took and
> transcriptions of executed documents; they stop entering the approval queue entirely.

The drain condition, stated: the queue empties when records stop being filed as proposals
and the four standing rules replace repeated adjudication. A queue of eight does not need
a new interface.

---

## What the four pairs have in common

Every bad version is legible to the stack and illegible to the reader. Every good version
costs the stack more work and the reader less. That trade is the entire point of the
format: the stack owns the bookkeeping, and Nick owns only the call that could not have
been made without him.
